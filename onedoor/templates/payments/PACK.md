# Worked examples: payments

A small set of onedoor policies that demonstrate the policy vocabulary against a
payments-shaped world: money out, a reversal, refunds, a human-confirmed batch, an
outbound webhook, and a read.

## What this is

**Worked examples.** They are concrete, they load, and every rule in them is exercised
against the real engine in `tests/templates/` with its verdict asserted — so what each
one *does* is recorded rather than described.

They exist to show what the vocabulary can express: tiers, caps, effect floors,
compensating commands, bounds, and URL-typed parameter matching, each on a rule where
that mechanism earns its place.

## What this is not, named rather than disclaimed

**This pack is not a compliance artifact, and nobody who wrote it has payments domain
authority.** A named gap is a service to the reader; a disclaimer is a service to the
writer, so here are the gaps by name. A payments practitioner will expect these and will
not find them:

- **Sanctions screening.** No rule here checks a counterparty against any list, and
  nothing in the engine knows what a list is.
- **KYC / customer due diligence.** Identity, verification status and risk tier are
  absent — this pack cannot express *"only for verified customers"* because nothing here
  models a customer.
- **Chargebacks and disputes.** The reversal here is an operational undo, not a dispute
  lifecycle: no representment, no evidence deadlines, no scheme rules.
- **Multi-currency settlement.** Every amount is euro. There is no FX, no rate source,
  no settlement timing, and the caps would silently mean something different under a
  second currency.
- **Regulatory reporting.** No reporting obligation, threshold or retention rule is
  modelled, in any jurisdiction.

Anything else a practitioner would expect and does not see here is also absent — this
list is what we know is missing, not a proof that nothing else is.

**Adopt these only after review by someone who can stand behind the domain.** The
numbers below are deliberately small so that a pack adopted without review fails closed
and noisily rather than permitting something expensive.

## Adjusting it

Every value is concrete and already safe. **Adjusting means editing a real number, not
filling a blank** — this pack contains no placeholders, and that is a rule rather than a
style choice. A blank is a promise that someone will remember; and a template with blanks
cannot be checked, because it is not yet the thing the check checks.

Two rules the pack holds itself to, and which your edits should keep:

1. **Every effect a rule names is declared.** An effect with no `effects:` entry behind
   it is silently dropped by the engine — no floor, no caps — so the label reads as a
   protection while doing nothing.
2. **No declared effect has a null floor.** A null `min_tier` beside an opaque-host block
   is how an opaque destination auto-executed once before.

## Adopting it

The documented path is the **ratification ceremony**, not a plain load:

```python
from onedoor.templates import payments
receipt = payments.adopt(conn, ratified_by_session="you@example", now=now_utc())
```

That produces a `ratifications` row, and its `candidate_digest` **is** the pack's own
`policy_digest` by construction — same models, same canonical form, same function. So
anyone holding this pack can recompute that value and match it against the receipt:
*"why does this policy allow X?"* resolves to *"this pack was ratified on that date"* by
recomputation rather than by a stored pointer.

`PACK_DIGEST` is the pack's **file** identity — is this byte-for-byte the pack that
shipped, comments included. It is generated by `python -m scripts.pack_digest`, never
typed.
